Context

Why the Design Looks the Way It Does

Bayun's design already incorporated my specific preferences — so when you execute it, knowing what I asked for helps you understand the intentionality behind every choice. Nothing in this design is a default. These are the things I told Bayun I wanted:

Closed mouth
No open jaw, no strike pose. The snake is calm, self-possessed — not threatening. The head is tucked and the mouth closed. This changes the entire energy of the piece.
Anatomy-first composition
The snake's loops should feel like they belong to the body — the waist, rear, and quad naturally frame the coils. The composition follows the body's contours rather than sitting on top of them.
Infinity knot as centerpiece
The figure-8 loop in the mid-lower section was specifically requested. It's not just a coil — it's a deliberate shape that carries meaning and creates one of the strongest visual anchors in the piece.
Consistent upward flow
The directional motion of the snake should be coherent — a consistent zig-zag moving upward, never chaotic or reversing on itself without reason. The elegance is in the controlled, intentional direction.
Large negative ring space on the quad
A big sweeping ring with open skin showing on the quad was something I specifically loved in Bayun's reference work and asked him to incorporate. That open negative space on the thigh is not leftover — it was requested.

02 — Technical Language

Bayun's Style DNA

These aren't stylistic preferences — they're structural. The elements that make a Bayun snake a Bayun snake.

01
Dual-Texture Scales
Two distinct scale treatments on the same snake. The dorsal (top) body: large, clearly defined diamond or rhombus-shaped scales. The ventral (belly) surface: tight horizontal parallel lines like compressed ribs. This contrast gives the snake its 3D dimensionality and separates the top of the body from the underside visually.
02
Deep Black Saturation
Shadow zones and coil folds go fully black — not grey, not softly shaded. True ink saturation in the deepest areas, then fine linework everywhere else. No muddy midtone range. The contrast is stark: deep black vs. precise fine lines. Nothing in between.
03
Deliberate Negative Space
The skin showing through the coils isn't leftover — it's designed. Large open loops create intentional voids that carry as much visual weight as the snake itself. The negative space has shape. Don't fill it.
04
Red Cord Accents
A signature in some pieces: fine red knotted rope threading around the snake. Sparse — it doesn't dominate. The red is the only color in the entire piece; everything else is black and grey. It appears in some of Bayun's work and not others. It's a choice, not a rule.
05
Wispy Motion Lines
Fine hair-like lines extend from the snake's head and tail, suggesting movement through air. Three to five lines, curving organically. They're sparse — never overdone. They make the snake feel alive rather than static on the skin.

02B — Shading

How Bayun Shades

The shading approach is as specific as the composition. These are the rules that give Bayun's snakes their dimensional, almost sculpted quality.

A
Per-Scale Internal Gradient
Every individual dorsal scale has its own internal shading. The darkest point is at the bottom edge of each scale — where it sits beneath the scale above it (cast shadow). Moving up toward the free tip of the scale, it lightens. This is repeated consistently across the entire body. The result is a "roof tile" depth effect — each scale reads as a 3D object, not just a drawn outline.
B
Hard Body Shadow Cuts
The cylindrical body has a clear highlight zone and a shadow zone. The shadow side doesn't fade gradually — it drops to deep black quickly, with a relatively hard transition. Where the body curves away from view, overlaps itself, or tucks into a coil fold, it goes to full black saturation. No long soft gradients. The contrast is bold and deliberate.
C
Belly / Ventral Plates
The ventral (belly) surface uses tight horizontal bands — not scales, but rib-like plates. Each band has its own shadow on the lower edge. The belly is a clearly different texture from the dorsal body — lighter overall, with fine line hatching rather than scale shapes. The transition from dorsal scales to ventral plates is a defined anatomical line.
D
Head Anatomy
The head uses the same per-scale shading logic, but the scales are larger and more individually prominent — each one more visible than on the body. The eye is a simple, clean circle — small, with a dark round pupil, no elaborate iris rendering. The forked tongue (if present) is two ultra-fine lines — barely there, trailing into space. No ornamentation on the face. Quiet and precise.
E
No Soft Midtone Washes
There's no grey wash or soft airbrush midtone range in Bayun's work. The tonal range is achieved entirely through line density and scale shading — not through grey ink or soft blending. The work is technically linear. Where it looks "grey" it's fine lines and dot clusters, not a wash. This keeps the piece crisp and prevents it from looking muddy as it ages.
F
Coil Twist Shadow Logic
When the snake body bends into a loop or twist, the curve itself generates the lighting — not an external light source.

The inside of the curve (the tightest part of the bend) drops to near-pure black — a hard, confident shadow shape. The apex of the curve (where the body faces outward) is the lightest point, with scale texture fully visible. The sides transition quickly from apex to shadow.

These inner-curve blacks become abstract shapes in their own right — bold dark voids that add visual weight and drama to every coil. The twist is the shading, not just a shape to shade.

03 — The Design

What to Preserve

Every compositional choice Bayun made for this piece is intentional. Here's what each one is and why it matters.

Composition

The Tapered Diamond Silhouette

The entire piece fits inside an elongated diamond shape — wide at the top where the leg is widest (hip and quad), narrowing to a point at the ankle. This mirrors the natural reverse taper of the leg itself. The silhouette of the composition and the silhouette of the body become the same shape. The piece belongs to the leg rather than sitting on top of it.

images/annotated1.pngSilhouette arrow diagram

Geometry

Triangle in the Upper Section

The upper portion of the design — the coils near the hip — forms a secondary triangle. Inside it, the snake's head is positioned to follow the natural diagonal of the oblique muscles. The head direction isn't arbitrary: it's aligned with the body beneath it. The piece reads as anatomically intentional, not placed.

The triangle formed by the infinity knot and the neck area is another secondary shape worth noting — there's interesting geometry throughout.

images/annotated2.pngTriangle + head direction diagram

Motion

The Möbius Effect

The snake's head overlaps its own body. This is a specific, deliberate choice: it creates a Möbius strip or infinite loop quality. Follow the snake's motion and you never find a beginning or end — the flow is continuous. It makes the piece feel alive in a way a snake with a clear start and end doesn't.

Note: My piece has a closed mouth. No open jaw, no menacing expression. The head is calm and tucked — this is not a strike pose.

images/annotated3.pngMöbius / infinite loop diagram

Structure

Infinity Knot + Negative Ring Space

The lower-mid section contains a clear figure-8 infinity knot — this is a designed shape, not just a coil. It's a centerpiece of the composition.

Throughout the piece, large ring shapes create open negative space voids. These aren't gaps — they're designed shapes. The circles of skin inside the coils are compositionally intentional. The top of the snake also curls back on itself as it ascends toward the ribs, adding another layer of looping self-referential energy.

images/design.jpgDesign — infinity knot in lower half

Flow

Consistent Directional Zig-Zag

The snake's overall motion follows a consistent upward zig-zag — not random, not chaotic. The zig-zag size increases proportionally as the leg gets wider going up. At the top, the composition shifts to going straight up to align with the transition from leg to hip.

Breathing room is maintained at the front edge — the snake never crowds the border. The comparison below shows coherent directional flow (left) versus directionless chaos (right). The left is the goal.
